The pros

The five pros in depth: serves, habits and a plan per level

The pros guide tells you what each character wants to do. This one shows the numbers behind it, so you can tell when a habit is a tendency and when it is nearly a rule, and it adds a plan for each CPU level.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Every pro is built from nine style numbers (the persona) between 0 and 1 (aggression, topspin, slice, drop shots, net play, serve, consistency, defence and speed), a short list of habits, and a small stat profile with a few strengths and one or two mild weaknesses. The figures below come from those numbers in the game’s code. Where a figure is a chance, it is the chance in an ordinary situation: pressure, ball height and where you stand shift it. Read how to beat each of the five pros first if you have not; this page assumes you know who is who, and the pros page has their looks and countries.

What the CPU level changes

The level sets how well the pro executes; the pro sets what they try to do. The same style at a different level plays very differently.

Two consequences. At Rookie the pro makes a lot of errors, so patience is a plan. At Pro the errors nearly vanish, so waiting for a mistake stops working and you have to take the ball early and make your own winners. And a Pro CPU leaves a ball that is only 20 cm out, so a ball landing just beyond the line will not be chased: do not expect a rescue from a slightly long ball. Serve speeds are before the pro’s own serve stat and gear, which move them a little.

How every pro serves

All five use the same serve routine, with their serve number tilting it. On a first serve each picks the T, the body or wide. The pros land close to these shares, with the big servers (Federli, Sinnari) hitting the body least:

  • The T: about 38 to 47 percent.
  • Wide: about 34 to 37 percent, rising to about 41 to 44 percent when the serve is a slider that curves away: a right-hander in the deuce court, a left-hander (Navidal) in the ad court.
  • The body: about 15 to 24 percent.

The habit worth knowing is that after two serves to the same spot in the same court, the chance of a third is cut to 30 percent of normal. After two T serves from the deuce side, expect wide or the body. A second serve is a different shot: it tops out around 140 km/h at any level (a little more once the pro’s stats and gear are added), with 40 percent at your body, 42 percent at your backhand side and 18 percent at your forehand side. Treat it as a ball to attack.

Serve and volley is rare: Federli follows about 3 first serves in 10 to the net, Alcarraza about 1 in 7, and the other three never do.

Nolan Jokavic: the retriever

Position: about 0.9 m behind the baseline, further back than anyone. Habits: the best return in the game (it cuts the pressure of a fast first serve by about a third), a lob at least a quarter of the time you come to the net even when he is not stretched, and he gives up on a lob only when it is more than half a second beyond his reach. He slices about a third of his backhands and comes in on no more than about 4 in 100 short balls. Weak spots: the least free power of the five and a plain serve (0.65).

  • Rookie: rally cross-court and let the errors come; he is mostly harmless.
  • Club: shorten points. Take the first ball after the serve, and approach on a deep ball so the lob is your only worry.
  • Pro: his reactions are the best of the five and his reach is long, so no passing shot is safe. Play to the open corner twice before finishing, and put your effort into his service games, which are his softer side.

Raul Navidal: the lefty with the loop

Position: about 0.7 m behind the baseline. Habits: his topspin number is 0.98, so he loops the ball high, and he aims at your backhand whenever he can. He almost never slices, hardly comes in, and chases everything. Weak spots: the gentlest serve of the five (0.55, with 4 percent less pace and accuracy than a neutral player) and the only pro with weaker volleys.

  • Rookie: stand your ground and hit through his loop; the scatter does the rest.
  • Club: meet the high ball early, or step well back, and use your own slice to take his bounce away.
  • Pro: he runs everything down, so aim to win the point at the net. Return his serve deep and look for the first short ball: his service game is where to attack.

Rolf Federli: the attacker

Position: about 0.2 m behind the baseline. Habits: he slices up to six in ten backhands and about a quarter of forehands, and comes forward on up to 7 in 10 genuinely short balls. He plays a drop shot on about 1 short ball in 8 when you stand deep, and follows 30 percent of first serves to the net. He gives up on a lob once it is about a fifth of a second beyond his reach. Weak spots: defence (0.66) is his lowest number, and his reactions and speed are slightly below neutral.

  • Rookie: keep the ball deep and let him over-attack; lobs are easy winners.
  • Club: return low and block it back; lob his first volley over the backhand shoulder.
  • Pro: his volleys and touch are real, so do not rely on a single pass. Move him with a deep ball, then lob. Long rallies are where he is weakest.

Gianni Sinnari: the power hitter

Position: about 0.8 m inside the baseline, which takes time from you and from himself. Habits: flat, early and aggressive. From any comfortable ball he goes for a flat winner about three times in ten, and he adds extra pace to his regular drives. He almost never slices or drops. Weak spots: the lowest consistency (0.62) and defence (0.60) of the five, a flat ball with little spin, and less touch.

  • Rookie: just keep the ball in; his errors are very frequent.
  • Club: give him high, slow balls and slice. He stands well inside the baseline, so a deep, high ball takes away the early timing he likes.
  • Pro: his scatter is about a third of what it is at Rookie, so the error-prone hitter becomes a real threat. Stay patient, hit deep and do not feed him pace. His serve is among the strongest, so block returns back deep.

Carlito Alcarraza: the artist

Position: about 0.5 m behind the baseline. Habits: his drop-shot number is 0.95. When you stand deep and a ball sits up, he plays a drop shot on about 4 short balls in 10, and in a rally he changes pace, spin or height on about one ball in five (a high loop, a low skid, a sharp angle or a flat drive). After his own drop shot he lobs more often than not. He comes in on up to 4 in 10 short balls and follows 14 percent of first serves to the net. Weak spots: 4 percent less control than a neutral player, so he is error-prone, and the second-lowest consistency (0.66).

  • Rookie: chase the drop and lob it back; his errors carry the match.
  • Club: stand on the baseline or no more than a step behind it (about 0.3 m). A drop shot on a short ball needs you deeper than that, so staying up takes away his best weapon.
  • Pro: his legs are quick and his hands the best (touch is his top stat), so hit deep and early, and answer a drop with depth, never with a soft ball he can lob over.
