Warming up for tennis and staying injury-free

Tennis asks a lot of the body: sudden starts and stops, rapid changes of direction and thousands of repeated swings. A short warm-up and a sensible build-up make the game safer and more enjoyable.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Warm up for 10 to 15 minutes: raise your pulse, move your joints through their range, practise tennis movements, then hit gently before you hit hard.
  • Do dynamic (moving) stretches before play and hold static stretches afterwards.
  • Most tennis injuries come from doing too much too soon, so build up your playing time gradually, and rest.
  • This is general information, not medical advice. For pain, an old injury or a health condition, ask a doctor, physiotherapist or qualified coach.

Why warm up

A warm-up raises your heart rate and body temperature, wakes up the muscles you are about to use and takes the joints through their range of movement. It also lets you practise the movements of the game at a low intensity, so the first sprint or the first serve is not a shock. A warm-up is widely recommended by coaches and sports medicine bodies as a way of preparing the body and reducing the chance of strains. It also means you are ready to play your best from the first game.

A warm-up in four parts

  1. Raise your pulse (about 3 minutes). Easy jogging, skipping, or side shuffles along the baseline. You should feel warmer and a little out of breath, but able to talk.
  2. Dynamic stretches (about 4 minutes). Controlled movements that take the joints through their range: leg swings forwards and sideways, walking lunges with a rotation of the trunk, arm circles forwards and back, trunk rotations, high knees and heel flicks. Move smoothly, with no bouncing and no forcing.
  3. Tennis movement (about 2 minutes). Split steps, side shuffles, a few short sprints and quick changes of direction, and a handful of slow shadow swings of the strokes.
  4. Easy hitting (about 5 minutes or more). Start with mini tennis from the service line, then rally from the baseline at half pace, then volleys. Leave the serve and overheads until your shoulder is warm, and start them at about half effort. Build up the speed gradually.

On a cold day, take longer. On a hot day, make it shorter but do not skip it.

Dynamic or static stretching?

Dynamic stretching uses movement: the leg swings and lunges above. Static stretching means holding a position, such as pulling a foot to your backside and holding it. Long held stretches right before play can leave muscles slightly less ready for quick, powerful movement, so many coaches prefer dynamic movements first and held stretches afterwards, when the muscles are warm. If you do hold a stretch, take it only to a feeling of mild tension, hold it for around 20 to 30 seconds, breathe normally and never bounce. Stretching should never hurt.

Looking after the shoulder

The serve and the overhead are the most demanding shots for the shoulder. Do not start a session with full-power serves. Begin at about half speed, and add a little each time, and stop when you are tired, because fatigue changes technique and raises the load on the shoulder. Many coaches and physiotherapists give tennis players simple strengthening exercises for the muscles around the shoulder blade, often with a light resistance band. A qualified coach or physiotherapist can show you ones that suit you. The serve technique in the serving article also helps, because a smooth motion with the legs doing part of the work is easier on the arm.

Build up gradually

Most overuse injuries come from doing too much too soon: playing four times in a week after a month off, adding a long session, or starting to serve a lot. Increase your playing time step by step, with rest days in between, and take extra care after a break or an illness. Strength work for the legs, core and shoulders, and some general fitness, are a good base for the game. Sleep and food count too. Learn to tell the difference between the tiredness of a good workout and the sharp, increasing or lingering pain that is a signal to stop.

Heat, water and sun

Drink water before, during and after play, and not just when you are already thirsty. In hot weather, take breaks in the shade, wear a hat and sun cream, and keep sessions shorter. If you feel dizzy, sick, confused or get a pounding headache, stop, cool down, and get help if it does not pass quickly. Do not play through it.

Cool down and check your gear

After play, walk or jog gently for a few minutes, drink, and do some gentle held stretches while the muscles are warm. Before play, check the basics: shoes in good condition and right for the surface (see court surfaces), a grip size that suits your hand and a racket that is not too heavy, as discussed in the equipment article, and a court with no wet patches or loose balls.

When to ask for help

Ask a doctor, physiotherapist or qualified coach if pain is sharp, lasts for more than a few days, wakes you at night, comes with swelling, numbness or weakness, or returns every time you play. Check with a doctor before starting if you have a health condition, have been inactive for a long time, or are unsure whether tennis is right for you.

Try it in Palm Court

The game is not exercise, but if you play with your body, warm up as well. Phone racket, Hand cam and Body cam all use real arm swings, so loosen your shoulders and wrists first, keep clear of furniture and people, and take breaks, especially during long sessions. The setup pages in the phone racket guide and the how to play page have the safety notes. In the game, treat your first few minutes the same way as on court: open Play, Training, Drills and play a round of Rally streak at an easy pace, or start with a Rookie tiebreak, before you take on a Pro. A harder swing is not a better swing in Palm Court either: the fastest shots miss most often, and a smooth, medium swing is kinder to your arm too.
