How to volley: net play basics

Volleys look easy until you try one. Done well they take time away from your opponent and end points quickly. The secret is that a volley is a very small shot.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A volley is hit before the ball bounces. Use a short, firm punch with no big backswing: the ball already has pace, so you only need to redirect it.
  • Use the continental grip for forehand and backhand volleys. There is no time to change grips at the net.
  • Keep the racket head up, above your wrist, and meet the ball in front of you with a firm wrist.
  • Come in behind a deep, good shot, split step, and do not stop in mid-court where balls bounce at your feet.

Why go to the net?

At the net you are close to the opponent's court, so you have more angles and the opponent has less time to react. A well-placed volley often ends the point, and a player who always stays at the baseline is easy to rally against. Net play also asks for different skills: quick hands, good judgement and the nerve to move forward. It is not for every point. The trick is to go in when you are in control and the opponent is in trouble, and to leave it alone when you are not.

Grip and ready position

Use the continental grip, the hammer grip described in the serving article. The racket face then opens slightly and stays square to a ball on either side without any adjustment. Hold the racket in front of you, with the head above the level of your hand and about as high as your eyes or chin, where you can see it. Keep both hands on the racket, the elbows in front of your body, and bend the knees with your weight on the balls of the feet. Practise moving the racket from there to the left and right without letting the head drop.

The volley stroke

  1. Split step. As your opponent hits, hop slightly and land with your feet wide as they strike the ball. It prepares you to move in any direction.
  2. Turn the shoulders a little. Take the racket back no further than your body. The turn is the backswing, which is why volleys need very little arm movement.
  3. Step towards the ball. Step with the opposite foot to the hand that is hitting: the left foot for a right-handed forehand volley, the right foot for a backhand volley. The step moves your weight into the shot.
  4. Punch. The racket moves forward and slightly down through the ball, with a firm wrist and a slightly open face. Meet the ball in front of you, where you can see it.
  5. Stop short. The follow-through is brief. Let the racket continue a little and then recover to the ready position, ready for the next ball.

A volley is not a swing. If you take a big backswing you will be late, and the racket will either hit the ball too hard or miss. On a fast ball the best volley is a block: hold the racket firmly in front of you and let the ball's pace do the work.

Where to stand

A common aim is to stand somewhere around halfway between the net and the service line. From there you are close enough to angle the ball away and far enough back to react to a lob. Standing too close to the net makes you easy to lob. Standing too far back means that every ball lands at your feet. The one place to avoid stopping is the middle of the court around the service line. A ball that lands there bounces at your feet, and you have to hit it from below the height of the net, which is difficult. If you are caught there, keep moving forward, or step back and play a groundstroke.

Approach shots and the first volley

Most volleys come after an approach shot: a groundstroke hit as you move forward. Choose a short ball, one that lands well inside the baseline, because then you have less distance to cover. Hit the approach deep and towards the opponent's weaker side, or into the open court, and keep moving forward behind it. Split step as the opponent hits the reply, then volley. The first volley is the hardest, because you are usually still some way back. Hit it deep, towards the corner away from your opponent. The second volley can go short or sharp, because the opponent is on the run.

Low, high and half volleys

  • Low volleys (below the top of the net): bend your knees, not your back, and open the racket face so you can lift the ball over the net. Aim deep and keep the racket head above your wrist.
  • High volleys (above shoulder height): punch down and forward, aiming at the open court. This is where you can finish the point.
  • Half volleys are hit just after the ball bounces, usually at your feet when you could not avoid it. Stay low, keep the racket face slightly open and use a short lift, as if scooping the ball.
  • Overhead smash. When the opponent lobs, turn sideways, point at the ball with your free hand, and swing like a serve. If the lob is deep, let it bounce first and smash it when it comes up again, which gives you time to set up. Never lose sight of the ball.

Common mistakes and fixes

Balls fly long or outA big swing, or hitting a fast ball too hardUse a block: a firm racket and no backswing.
Balls go into the netRacket head dropped below the wristKeep the head up and bend your knees to reach low balls.

You are lobbed again and againStanding too close to the netStand a step or two further back and learn to smash.
Balls land at your feetStopping in mid-courtKeep moving to the net after a good approach.
Passed down the lineStanding in the middle when the opponent is wideMove a step towards the side the opponent is hitting from, to cover the line they are likely to aim at.

Drills

  • Volley-volley. Stand a few metres from your partner, both inside the service line, and rally with soft volleys. Count how many you can keep going.
  • Wall volleys. Stand about three metres from a wall and volley continuously, keeping the racket up and the swing short.
  • Approach and volley. A partner feeds you a short ball. Hit an approach, move in, split step, and volley two balls, first deep and then to the open court.
  • Smash practice. A partner lobs gently, and you point, turn and smash, letting some bounce.
  • Alternating sides. A partner feeds balls alternately to your forehand and backhand sides, to build the habit of the firm wrist and the split step.

Try it in Palm Court

In the game the volley is a shot of its own, and the label after your swing names it: a short, soft swing is a block volley, a firmer one a punch volley, a very soft one with backspin a drop volley, and a hard swing at a ball above head height a smash. Good timing makes a volley tighter, just as in real tennis. Your player does the running, so what you practise is the decision: block the fast ball, punch the easy one, smash the lob. Rolf Federli is the net player of the five pros, and playing as him (level 10, or 2,000 Fuzz) on grass is the quickest way to see volleys in action. The net play guide covers how the game works out these shots, and the pros page introduces each character.
