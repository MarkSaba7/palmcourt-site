Choosing a racket, strings and balls

You do not need an expensive racket to learn tennis. You need one that fits your hand, is not too heavy and is strung sensibly. This guide explains what the numbers on a racket mean and what to look for.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most adult beginners are well served by a racket with a head of about 100 to 110 square inches, a weight in the middle range and a flexible, comfortable frame.
  • Grip size matters more than most people think. Too small a grip makes you squeeze, and a squeeze strains the arm.
  • Choose soft, forgiving strings such as multifilament or synthetic gut, and be wary of stiff polyester until you have a developed game.
  • Try before you buy, and do not copy the racket of an advanced player: those are small, heavy and demanding.

Parts of a racket

A racket's specification lists a handful of numbers. Here is what each one means for a beginner.

Head size

The head size is the area of the string bed, quoted in square inches or square centimetres. A bigger head gives a larger sweet spot, the part of the strings that sends the ball back cleanly, and a little more power, so it is more forgiving of mishits. A smaller head gives more control to a player who already hits the middle of the strings. Most adult beginner rackets have heads of about 100 to 110 square inches (roughly 645 to 710 square centimetres). Players who have been playing for years often move to smaller heads, but there is no need to rush.

Weight and balance

Weight is usually given for the unstrung frame, in grams or ounces. Many adult recreational rackets weigh between about 255 and 300 grams (roughly 9 to 10.5 ounces). A lighter racket is easy to swing but passes more shock to the arm and is pushed around by the ball. A heavier one is more stable and powerful, but tiring, and a racket that is too heavy is a common cause of sore arms. A frame that feels easy to swing for a full session is a better choice than a heavier one that looks impressive. Balance is where the weight sits. A head-light racket feels nimble, especially for volleys and serving, and a head-heavy one gives more power with less effort.

Length

The standard adult length is 27 inches (68.6 centimetres). Some rackets are a little longer, up to the legal maximum of 29 inches (73.7 centimetres), which adds leverage on the serve but can feel unwieldy. Children use shorter rackets, in sizes such as 19, 21, 23, 25 and 26 inches, chosen by height and age, so they can swing properly. A coach or shop can help choose.

Grip size

The grip size is the distance round the handle, usually numbered from 0 or 1 up to 5, or about 10 to 12 centimetres. Hold the racket in an eastern forehand grip: if you can just fit the index finger of your other hand in the gap between your fingertips and the heel of your palm, the size is about right. If the handle is too small, you squeeze it hard to stop it turning, and a hard squeeze is a common contributor to a tired, strained forearm. If you are between two sizes, choose the smaller one and add an overgrip, a thin wrap, to build it up.

Stiffness and string pattern

Stiffness (often given as an RA number) describes how much the frame bends. A lower number is more flexible and tends to feel softer and kinder to the arm, while a stiffer frame sends the ball back with more power but passes more shock to the arm. String pattern is the number of strings: an open pattern, such as 16 mains by 19 crosses, has bigger gaps, so the strings move more, giving more spin and power. A denser pattern, such as 18 by 20, gives more control and lasts longer.

Tension is how tightly the strings are pulled, in pounds or kilograms. The frame usually prints a recommended range, often somewhere around 50 to 60 pounds (23 to 27 kilograms). Lower tension gives more power and comfort, and higher tension more control. A beginner is usually best in the middle or lower half of the range. Gauge is the thickness: a lower number is thicker and lasts longer, and a higher one is thinner with more feel.

Strings lose tension even when they are not used, so replace them. A common rule of thumb is to restring as many times a year as you play each week, and sooner if they break or the racket starts to feel dead.

Balls

  • Pressurised balls are lively, but lose their pressure within a few weeks of opening the can, and they feel flat. Keep spare ones sealed.
  • Pressureless balls last far longer, but feel harder and heavier, so they suit machines and casual hitting more than a regular match.
  • Duty: regular-duty balls have a softer felt and are common on clay, and extra-duty balls have a tougher felt for hard courts, where the surface wears the felt faster.
  • Slower balls for learners. Lower-compression balls, sold in red, orange and green, bounce lower and slower and are used with children. Adult beginners can use them too, because the extra time makes the strokes easier to learn.

Shoes and the extras

Tennis shoes are built to support side-to-side movement, unlike running shoes, which are made for going forwards. They are worth putting first in the budget, because your ankles and knees depend on them. Choose a sole to match the surface, as in the article on court surfaces. An overgrip is a cheap way to refresh a handle and absorb sweat. Vibration dampeners are often said to change little more than the sound of the strings. Bring water to every session, and a hat or sun cream outdoors.

Buying sensibly

  • Try before you buy. Many clubs and shops lend demo rackets. Hit with two or three and see which feels easy.
  • Pre-strung rackets are fine for beginners and cheaper. A good racket is not a requirement for learning.
  • Ask a coach. Someone who has watched you hit knows what suits you.
  • Avoid copying advanced players. Their rackets are small, heavy and stiff, and they are made for people who swing very fast.
  • Buy a second-hand racket if you are unsure. Check the frame for cracks and replace the strings.
