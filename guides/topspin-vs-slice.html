Topspin or slice: when to use each

Almost every shot you hit has some spin. Understanding what it does to the ball tells you which shot to play, and why a plain flat shot is the hardest one to keep in the court.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Topspin makes the ball dip down into the court, so you can hit it high over the net and still land it deep. It then bounces up and forward.
  • Slice (backspin) keeps the ball flatter and lower, and it bounces low and slows down. It is a defensive and change-of-pace shot.
  • Topspin is your default rally shot. Slice is for defence, approaches, drop shots and breaking the rhythm.
  • Spin comes from racket speed and swing path, not from a harder hit. The path decides the kind of spin; the speed decides how much.

What spin does to the ball

A spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. The air flows differently over each side of the ball, and the difference pushes the ball: downwards for topspin, where the top of the ball spins towards your opponent, and upwards for backspin, where the top of the ball turns back towards you. That is why topspin balls arc and dip, while slice balls look flatter and seem to hang in the air.

The bounce changes too. A topspin ball grips the court as it lands and shoots up and forward, so the opponent has to deal with a high, fast bounce. A slice ball bounces low and loses speed, and on a slippery court it skids on through. The same shot played on different courts behaves differently; the surfaces article looks at that.

How to hit topspin

  1. Grip. An eastern or semi-western forehand grip, or a two-handed backhand, closes the racket face a little and suits a brushing swing.
  2. Drop the racket. Let the head fall below the height of the ball, with your knees bent so you can get under it.
  3. Swing low to high. Brush up the back of the ball and finish above the opposite shoulder. The racket face is roughly vertical or slightly closed at contact, and the path is what spins the ball.
  4. Aim higher over the net. A topspin shot should clear the net by a comfortable margin, such as a metre, and then drop in. That margin is what makes topspin forgiving.

Many beginners swing level and hit through the ball. The ball goes flat and fast, and either hits the net or flies long. Think about brushing the ball with a fast racket head, not pushing it.

How to hit slice

  1. Grip. Continental, which lets the racket face open naturally.
  2. Turn and take the racket back high, with the free hand on the throat.
  3. Swing down and forward through the back and underside of the ball, with the face a little open. Think of it as a smooth push through the ball with a downward edge, like cutting a thin slice from its back.
  4. Finish forward and low, staying balanced and keeping your head still at contact.

The common faults are a very steep chop, which makes the ball pop up, and a face that is too open, which makes it sail long. A good slice is low over the net, deep, and has enough forward pace to stay in play.

When to use topspin

SituationWhy topspin helps
Rallying from the baselineA high margin over the net and a ball that lands deep without going out.
Passing shots and anglesThe dip lets you hit hard across the court and still keep the ball in.
Pushing the opponent backA heavy, high bounce makes the opponent hit above their comfortable height.
Second serve and short balls to attackSpin lets you swing freely and still get the ball down.
Passing a player at the netA dipping ball at the feet is hard to volley.

When to use slice

  • When you are stretched. A slice floats, and the extra time lets you get back into position.
  • To change the pace. After a run of heavy topspin, a low slice can break your opponent's rhythm, especially if they like to hit hard.
  • As an approach shot. A slice stays low, which makes it hard to attack and gives you time to reach the net.
  • For drop shots. Backspin is what makes the ball sit down after the bounce.
  • Against tall players or high strike zones. A low ball makes them bend and lift it, which is harder than hitting at shoulder height.
  • On fast, low surfaces, where a sliced ball skids even lower.

A slice should be deep. A floating slice that lands short and sits up is an invitation to be attacked.

Mixing spins

The idea is not to choose one for good but to use both with a purpose. A typical pattern: slice low to the opponent's weaker side to draw a weak reply, then step in and hit topspin to the open court. Or rally with heavy topspin until the opponent is pushed back behind the baseline, then slip in a short, low slice. Variety makes opponents uncomfortable because they never see the same ball twice.

Common mistakes

  • Flat when you meant topspin. The swing path was level, not low to high. Check that your racket head drops under the ball.
  • Too little net clearance. Aim at least a metre above the net while you learn, and the ball will drop in.
  • Over-slicing. Slicing every ball gives the opponent the same pattern and the same time to prepare. Mix it up.
  • Using the wrist. Spin comes from the swing path and racket speed, not a flick of the wrist at contact.

Drills

  • Net clearance. Imagine a line a metre above the net, or pick a point on the fence behind it, and see how many of ten topspin groundstrokes pass above it and still land in.
  • Depth target. Place a cone or a towel about a metre inside the baseline and hit ten deep topspin balls at it, then ten slices.
  • Alternate. In a cross-court rally, alternate one slice and one topspin each shot.
  • Brush drill. From a short distance, a partner drops balls and you brush up each one so it spins as it lands, watching how it bounces.
