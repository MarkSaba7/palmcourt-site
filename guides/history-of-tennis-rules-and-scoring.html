A short history of tennis rules and scoring

Tennis keeps some very old habits: the strange counting, the word love, even the way the court is marked. This page follows the rules from the games of medieval Europe to the line-calling cameras of today, keeping to what historians are sure about.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Modern tennis grew out of older racket-and-ball games, above all real tennis, a walled, indoor game that is still played.
  • The count of 15, 30 and 40 came from real tennis. Nobody knows for certain why the numbers are what they are, or where the word love comes from.
  • Lawn tennis was patented in 1874, held its first championships in 1877 and took the court shape of today within a few years.
  • The biggest rule changes since are modern: the Open Era (1968), the tiebreak (early 1970s) and electronic line calling (2000s onwards).

Before lawn tennis: real tennis

People have hit balls over a line or against a wall for a very long time, but the direct ancestor of tennis is a game played in medieval France, usually called jeu de paume, the "game of the palm", because players first struck the ball with the palm of the hand. Gloves, and later rackets, replaced the bare hand, and the game spread across Europe. It was played indoors, in an enclosed court with walls and sloping side roofs that were part of the play, and a net or cord across the middle. We now call it real tennis (in some countries court tennis), to tell it apart from the game on lawns. A small number of real tennis courts are still in use today, and its rules are far more elaborate than those of the game most of us play.

Why 15, 30 and 40?

Lawn tennis borrowed its way of counting from real tennis, where the scores 15, 30 and 40 were already in use. Beyond that the history is hazy, and it is worth being honest about it. Several explanations are repeated, the best known being that the numbers came from the quarters of a clock face, with 45 shortened to 40 when spoken. Historians have not been able to prove that or any other. The same goes for love, meaning zero. A popular story says it comes from the French l'oeuf, an egg, which looks like a zero, and another says it comes from playing for love, meaning for nothing. Neither has been proved. Deuce is usually traced to a French word for two, since two points in a row are then needed. What is clear is that the counting is older than the sport we play, and that is why it does not look like arithmetic.

1874: a game for the lawn

In the 1870s a British army officer, Major Walter Clopton Wingfield, put together a version of the game for garden lawns. In 1874 he patented it and sold it as a boxed set with rackets, balls, a net and the rules. He gave the game a Greek-sounding name, Sphairistike, which did not catch on, and people soon called it lawn tennis. His court was a different shape from today's, narrower at the net than at the baselines, like an hourglass. One ingredient helped make it possible: the hollow rubber ball, which bounced well on grass.

The rules settle down

The new game was changing quickly, and clubs wanted a common set of rules. By the late 1870s the rectangular court took its modern size: 78 feet (23.77 metres) long and 27 feet (8.23 metres) wide for singles. The first championships for the new game were held in London in 1877. In those early years, players commonly served underarm. Overarm serving took over, as players found it gave far more pace and control, and that change shaped the serve we know today. The court markings, the service boxes and the number of serves allowed changed in the years that followed, but the shape of the game had been set.

A world sport

The game spread quickly from Britain to Europe, North America and Australia. Tennis was part of the first modern Olympic Games in 1896, left the programme for decades and returned as a medal sport in 1988. In 1913 the national associations formed the International Lawn Tennis Federation, now the International Tennis Federation, which still publishes the official Rules of Tennis. The next big change came in 1968, with the start of the Open Era: until then the great tournaments were for amateurs only, and from 1968 professionals and amateurs could play in the same events. That is why records are often quoted as belonging to the Open Era.

Equipment changed the game

The rules of tennis say little about the racket, so equipment has changed the sport without any change to the rules. The old wooden frames, with small heads, gave way to metal frames and then to lighter, stiffer frames of graphite and other composites, and heads grew larger. A bigger, stiffer, lighter racket lets players swing faster and add more spin, so the game became quicker and more powerful, and the strokes changed with it. Balls changed too. In the 1970s, yellow balls became standard, because they are far easier to see, especially on colour television. If you want to see how racket choices work today, the equipment article explains them.

The tiebreak and the modern count

For nearly a century the only way to finish a set was to win by two games, which could mean a very long match. An American, James Van Alen, devised the tiebreak as a way of ending a set that had reached a deadlock, and it was brought into the major tournaments in the early 1970s. In the form most used today, a set that reaches 6-6 is decided by a first-to-seven tiebreak that must be won by two points, and a set can then finish 7-6. Other formats grew up alongside it: a longer first-to-ten tiebreak in place of a deciding set in doubles and at clubs, no-ad scoring that settles a deuce with one point, and short sets for beginners. The scoreboard article shows how those are scored and who serves in each.

Calling the lines

For a long time every ball was judged by people: line judges on the court and an umpire in the chair. Disputed calls could be heated, and in the 2000s the professional tour began using electronic line calling, which tracks the ball and shows a replay of where it landed. Players can ask for a review of a call, and in recent years some tournaments have replaced human line judges with automatic live calling. Club tennis still relies on players calling the balls on their side, as it always has, with the long-standing rule that a ball touching any part of the line is good.

What has not changed

For all the new technology, the heart of the game would be familiar to its first players: the net across the middle, the diagonal service box, two chances to serve, and the odd count from love to forty. The basic rules have changed remarkably little. The history also explains why the count looks as it does, and why a player can come back from 0-40 down: a game still has to be won by two clear points. For a plain account of how points become games and sets, see tennis scoring explained.

Try it in Palm Court

The game keeps the old counting and the new formats side by side. The umpire calls "love", "fifteen", "thirty", "forty", "deuce" and "advantage", with the server's score first, and you can switch the spoken calls on or off in Settings, Audio. In Play, Training, Quick Match vs CPU, the Match setup page offers the modern Tiebreak (first to seven points, two clear), a Short set (first to four games) and a Full set (first to six games, with the usual tiebreak). The three surfaces are a small museum of the game too: grass (level 6) is where lawn tennis began, and you can compare it with clay (level 3) and hard courts. The line review is the modern part: on a close call the game replays the ball's mark against the line and tells you how far out or in it was, in centimetres. You can start a match from the game page, and the how to play page explains the swing.
