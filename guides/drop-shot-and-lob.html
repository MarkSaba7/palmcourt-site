The drop shot and the lob

Hard hitting wins plenty of points, but opponents get used to pace. The drop shot and the lob change the shape of the rally: one pulls the opponent forward, the other sends them back.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Both shots work because they are a surprise. Prepare exactly as you would for a groundstroke so the opponent cannot tell what is coming.
  • Play a drop shot from inside the court, from a ball you can reach comfortably, when the opponent is standing deep. Never from a stretched position.
  • Lob when the opponent is close to the net or when you need time. A lob must be high enough to clear their reach and deep enough not to be smashed.
  • Always have a plan for the next ball. A drop shot is often answered, and a short lob is a gift.

Why touch shots are worth learning

A player who only hits hard gives the opponent one thing to prepare for. Add a drop shot and a lob and the opponent must watch for both: they cannot stand so far back that a drop shot beats them, and they cannot crowd the net without fearing the lob. You do not need to play them often. A few well-chosen ones make every other shot work better, and they are also useful when the opponent has been pushing you around from the baseline and you need a different tool.

The drop shot: when to play it

  • The opponent is deep. Standing well behind the baseline, they have a long way to run forward.
  • You are balanced and inside the court. A short ball that sits up at about waist height is ideal, because you can prepare calmly and the ball will not be going away from you.
  • The opponent has just been moved. After a hard, deep ball that pushed them back, or when they are tired, they are slowest to turn and run forward.
  • After a long rally of heavy pace. The change of rhythm is itself the weapon.

Do not play a drop shot when you are stretched or off balance, when the ball is deep in your own court, or when the opponent is already close to the net or very quick. A drop shot that sits up is easy to punish with a winner, so the error cost is high. Clay courts are known for drop shots because the surface grips backspin and the ball loses pace on the bounce, but the position of your opponent matters more than the surface.

How to hit a drop shot

  1. Disguise it. Use the same turn, the same early racket preparation and the same start of the swing as for a normal groundstroke. If the opponent can see it coming they will already be running.
  2. Soften the hand. At the last moment slow the racket down. Hold the racket a little more loosely and let the face open so it cuts under the back of the ball.
  3. Brush down and under. The motion is a gentle, short slice, with the racket moving slightly down and forward through the ball. The backspin and the lack of pace make the ball drop quickly after the net and stay short after the bounce.
  4. Aim for a landing close to the net, a metre or two past it, and towards a sideline rather than the middle. A ball that lands near the sideline pulls the opponent out of position as well as forward.
  5. Move in behind it. Step forward after hitting, because a good opponent may well reach the ball and play it back short or over your head.

The drop shot also exists at the net, where it is called a drop volley: a soft, backspin volley that lands just over the net. See the volley article for that one.

Answering a drop shot

You will not always read it, but there are signs: a shorter backswing, a slower racket, an opponent who has just moved inside the baseline. Start moving forward as soon as you see the ball drop short and not after it has bounced twice. When you get there, do not just push it back short, because you will be attacked. Play it deep to the corner away from the opponent, or play your own drop shot or a lob if they have followed their shot into the net.

The lob: two kinds

A lob is a shot hit high over the opponent's head. There are two quite different types.

  • The defensive lob is high and deep. Its purpose is time. When you are stretched out wide, or the opponent has a strong attack, a high ball landing near the baseline lets you get back into position and the opponent has to turn from attack to defence.
  • The offensive lob is flatter and quicker, often with topspin, and just high enough to clear the reach of a player at the net. It is a surprise shot, and a good one wins the point outright because the opponent cannot recover.

When to lob

  • The opponent is at the net and you cannot pass them. A lob forces them to turn and run backwards.
  • You are in trouble and need time. A high lob to the middle of the court is a sensible reset.
  • The opponent has a weak overhead, or is facing the sun.
  • After a drop shot, the opponent who ran forward is often too far in to chase a lob.
  • In doubles, against a team that has both players near the net.

Avoid lobbing when a strong wind is blowing in the direction of the lob, because it will carry the ball out. Avoid it against a player with a powerful overhead who is standing back, and do not lob when a simple pass would win the point.

How to hit a lob

  1. Defensive lob: turn, then bend your knees and open the racket face a little. Swing from low to high, finishing high so the ball goes up, not forward. Think of aiming at the sky above the opponent's backhand shoulder, and the ball landing deep in the court.
  2. Offensive lob: keep the preparation of a normal groundstroke, then at the last moment brush up the ball with topspin and a slightly shorter, faster swing. The trajectory is lower than the defensive lob, so the ball has to pass just above the opponent's racket reach and then drop quickly.
  3. Direction: lob over the opponent's weaker side, usually the backhand, and away from where they are leaning. It is harder to smash a ball that is going across and behind them.
  4. Depth: a lob that lands short can be smashed. A ball that lands within the last metre or two before the baseline is the target.

Combining the two

The pairing is natural. A drop shot drags the opponent forward and, if they get to it, they are close to the net and vulnerable to a lob. Or a lob pushes them back and, if they retrieve it, they are far behind the baseline and the drop shot is on. Players who mix the two in a rally leave the opponent guessing. Keep your own position in mind: you will need to be balanced and watching for a reply after either.

LobToo short, so it is smashedHit higher and deeper, aiming over the backhand shoulder.
LobToo long, sailing outKeep the swing smooth and aim slightly shorter in wind.
BothOverusing themPlay one or two a game, when the situation fits.

Drills

  • Drop shots from the service line. A partner feeds from the net, and you play ten gentle drop shots into the service box, then move back a few steps.
  • Drop and cover. Play a drop shot, move in, and play one more ball against a partner who returns it.
  • Lob targets. Mark a zone near the baseline with cones. A partner stands at the net; lob ten balls so they land in the last two metres.
  • Defensive lobs from the corners. A partner feeds balls wide; hit high, deep lobs and recover to the middle.
  • Drop-lob pairs. Alternate a drop shot and a lob in a rally with a patient partner, so you feel what each does.

Try it in Palm Court

Both shots are in the game's controls. A drop shot is a gentle swipe coming down with the mouse, D on the keyboard, or L1 + □ (LB + X) on a controller. A lob is E on the keyboard, or △ / Y on a controller. The shot label names what you played. A drop shot is a real risk in the game, as in tennis: struck from the baseline it goes into the net now and then, the more so with bad timing or a hard ball to reach, and one that lands deep in the court sits up, so the CPU runs it down and attacks it (or lobs you, if you came in behind it). Only a drop that dies close to the net, away from the opponent, wins the point, and a CPU that sees several in a short time starts earlier and answers with a high, deep ball. Start a Quick Match against the Rookie CPU and wait for a moment when your opponent is far behind the baseline and you have a comfortable ball; then try one drop shot, and see whether you can follow it up. Carlito Alcarraza, the fifth pro, plays exactly this pair against you, so he is the best teacher.
