The tennis backhand: one hand or two

The backhand is the stroke most beginners dread, because it is on the side of the body that feels weaker. It becomes much easier once you pick a version that suits you and learn its few key habits.

Written by the Palm Court team · Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Neither backhand is better. Both are used at the top of the game, so choose the one that feels natural after a few sessions with each.
  • A two-hander is usually the easier one to learn and is steadier on fast balls. A one-hander gives more reach and a simpler slice, but asks more of your timing.
  • In both versions the keys are the same: turn the shoulders early, meet the ball in front of you, and swing from low to high.
  • Learn a one-handed slice either way. It is the best answer to a ball you cannot hit with topspin.

Why the backhand feels awkward

On the forehand side your racket arm swings away from your body and has plenty of room. On the backhand it swings across your body, and the shoulder gets in the way unless you turn it out of the way first. That is the secret of a good backhand: the turn matters even more than on the forehand. If you wait until the ball has bounced to turn, you will be late and cramped, and the stroke will turn into an arm push.

The two-handed backhand

For most players it is the natural starting point. Two hands give the racket stability against fast balls and let the non-dominant arm provide a lot of the power.

  • Grip. For a right-hander the right hand holds the lower part of the handle in a continental grip, or one bevel round towards the eastern backhand grip. The left hand sits above it in an eastern forehand grip, just as a left-hander would hold a forehand. The hands touch, with no gap between them.
  • Turn. Turn the shoulders until your back is half-turned to the net, taking the racket back with both hands.
  • Swing. Drop the racket head below the ball and swing from low to high. Picture it as a forehand hit by your left hand, with your right hand guiding. The left arm does the main work.
  • Contact. Meet the ball in front of the front hip, with both arms extended but not locked. Two-handers have less reach than one-handers, so use your feet to get close enough to the ball.
  • Finish. The racket continues up and round, often wrapping over the shoulder, and your chest ends facing the net.

The one-handed backhand

  • Grip. The eastern backhand grip, with the base knuckle of the index finger on the top bevel of the handle (see the grip picture in the forehand article). A continental grip works for slice. The wrist must stay firm, so this grip rewards patience.
  • Turn. The free hand holds the throat of the racket during the take-back, then lets go. Turn the shoulders fully, so that your back is almost to the net.
  • Swing. Low to high again, the arm extending forward and up while the free arm swings back for balance.
  • Contact. This is the key difference. The ball has to be met well in front of the body, with the arm extended and firm. Contact beside the body is far too late for a one-hander.
  • Finish. High, with the hitting arm stretched out in front and the racket pointing up.

If you cannot decide, spend a few sessions with each. Hit twenty backhands one-handed and twenty two-handed, and notice which one you get into the court more often and which one feels relaxed. If either hurts your wrist or elbow, say so to a coach before you carry on. The choice is not permanent, and many players use two hands for topspin and one for slice.

The slice backhand

This is the one-handed stroke that every player should learn, whichever topspin backhand they use. Use the continental grip and turn the shoulders as before, with the free hand on the throat. Take the racket back high, then swing down and forward through the ball with a slightly open face, as if cutting a thin slice off the back of the ball. The hitting arm extends and the finish stays low and forward, not wrapped round the body. A good slice stays low, has backspin and lands deep. It gives you time when you are stretched and a safe way to approach the net. The article on topspin and slice explains when to use it.

Footwork and the high ball

The footwork for the backhand is the same as for the forehand: split step as your opponent hits, turn on the first step, and take small adjusting steps so the ball arrives at the right distance. Try to step across towards the ball with your front foot, which brings your body weight into the shot. High balls are the usual trouble spot, especially for one-handers. Either let the ball drop to a more comfortable height by backing up, hit it early on the rise, or play a firm slice. Do not reach up and swing at a ball above the shoulder with a stiff arm.

The ball jams the bodyTurning too late, or standing too closeTurn on the first step and give yourself an arm's length of space.
Weak, floaty shotsAll arm, no body rotationRotate the shoulders back, then let the legs and trunk drive the swing.

One-hander: the elbow bends and the shot spraysContact too close to the bodyReach the ball further in front and keep the arm firm and extended.
Racket falls behind at contactLate contactStart the turn earlier and shorten the backswing.

Drills

  • Shadow turns. Practise the shoulder turn alone, ten times, holding the position for a second.
  • Drop-feed backhands. A partner drops balls just in front of you from close range. Hit slowly and aim at a target, like a spot in the service box.
  • Cross-court backhand rally. From the two baselines, trade backhands diagonally with a partner and count rallies of five, then ten.
  • Slice and topspin switching. Alternate one slice and one topspin on every ball, to learn both and to feel when each fits.
  • Wall backhands. Alone against a wall, keep the ball going with controlled backhands and watch your balance.
